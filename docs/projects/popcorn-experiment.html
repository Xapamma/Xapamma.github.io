Popcorn Popping Time Experiment

Author

Nan Jiang, Savanna (Kerby) McLelland, Cole Gwilliam, and John Holman

Published

December 9, 2025

Project overview

This experiment examined whether oil type and the timing of adding popcorn kernels affect total popping time. The study compared olive, vegetable, and coconut oil under two starting procedures. It was designed to distinguish the effects of each factor and determine whether the effect of kernel timing varied by oil type.

Key finding: The report concluded that the heated-start procedure took longer than the cold-start procedure (p = 0.00441). Oil type and the interaction between oil type and kernel timing were not statistically significant at the 0.05 level.

Study design

The team used a balanced, randomized 2 × 3 factorial design:

Factor Conditions
Oil type Olive, vegetable, and coconut
Kernel timing Cold start and heated start

Each of the six treatment combinations was repeated five times, for 30 trials in total. The response variable was the time to complete popping, measured in seconds. Treatment order was randomized before data collection to reduce potential bias from trial order.

For a cold-start trial, the kernels were added to the oil at the beginning of heating. For a heated-start trial, four kernels were added first; the remaining kernels were added after the first of those kernels popped. Trials followed a consistent stovetop procedure, including a standard oil amount, medium-high heat, and a defined stopping point based on the interval between pops.

Analysis and results

A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the effects of oil type, kernel timing, and their interaction on total popping time. At a significance level of α = 0.05, the results were:

Effect p-value Interpretation
Kernel timing 0.00441 Statistically significant
Oil type 0.06552 Not statistically significant
Oil type × kernel timing 0.09184 Not statistically significant

The report concluded that the heated-start procedure took longer than the cold-start procedure. The study did not find sufficient evidence that popping time differed by oil type, or that the effect of kernel timing depended on the oil used. These non-significant findings do not establish that the effects are exactly zero.

The team reviewed residual diagnostic plots and reported that the residuals were approximately normal, the randomized order supported independence, and the spread across groups was sufficiently similar for the equal-variance assumption to be reasonable. A log transformation was considered but did not improve the residual spread, so the untransformed model was retained. The reported power calculation, based on the observed data, exceeded 90%.

Interpretation and limitations

Because treatment order was randomized and the procedure was controlled, the results support causal conclusions for this specific stovetop setup. They should not be generalized to all popcorn, cooking equipment, or heating conditions. Differences in burner temperature, pan cooldown, and other uncontrolled aspects of the setup may have contributed to variation between trials.

Further work could use temperature-controlled equipment, allow more time between trials, increase the number of replications, and examine additional outcomes such as unpopped kernels, popped volume, or burning.
